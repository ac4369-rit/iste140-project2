The Money Chain: From Listener to Artist

Streaming platforms do not negotiate individual royalty deals with every artist because transaction costs would be prohibitive. Instead, money generated by subscriptions and advertising moves through a layered web of agreements and intermediaries across two distinct rights streams—the sound recording (master) and the musical composition—with actual payouts differing substantially between songwriters, signed or independent performers, and backing/session musicians.

  1. Streaming Platform (DSP): Collects subscription fees and ad revenue from both sides of the market, generally placing roughly two-thirds of music revenue into a shared pool (segmented by territory, tier, and payout model such as pro-rata, artist-centric, or fan-powered distribution) while retaining its operational share. Because streaming operates internationally, total payouts also reflect currency exchange differences and variations in national copyright law.
  2. Record Label or Digital Distributor (Master Rights): Master recording royalties flow to the rights owner—either a record label (under contracts governing advances, recoupment, and royalty splits) or a digital distributor (for independent artists). A distributor ingests the track with its metadata and ISRC, collects master payouts from DSPs, deducts its commission or fee (assumed here at 15%), and passes the remainder to the artist. When an artist is signed to a broad 360-degree contract, the label may also participate across wider revenue streams beyond recordings, such as live performance income and merchandise.
  3. Songwriters and Music Publishers (Composition Rights): Songwriters initially hold the copyright in their musical compositions. Depending on the deal, a songwriter may enter anything from a broad assignment of rights over a catalog to a non-exclusive administration agreement—or have rights handled within broader label/360-degree arrangements. When a song is recorded, the label licenses permission from the publisher or songwriter, while specialized digital licensing bodies such as IMPEL (Independent Music Publishers' E-Licensing) represent digital publishing rights for independent publisher members.
  4. Collective Management Organisations / CMOs (e.g., HDS ZAMP in Croatia): Songwriters and publishers join CMOs to manage public performance and mechanical rights, collect usage data and license fees, deduct administrative and statutory/cultural allocations, and distribute royalties to members and international partner societies. In Croatia, HDS ZAMP (Hrvatsko društvo skladatelja – Zaštita autorskih muzičkih prava) collects digital/online streaming revenues alongside broadcast, live, and other rights, reporting over €4.8 million in online/digital collections (15% of total collections, up 53% year-over-year) within €32.7 million in total 2025 authors' rights revenues.

Copyright, Contractual Friction, and Revenue Asymmetry

International copyright rules such as the World Intellectual Property Organisation (WIPO) Internet Treaties—the WIPO Copyright Treaty (WCT) and the WIPO Performers and Phonograms Treaty (WPPT)—established the "making available" right, covering on-demand access where listeners choose when to stream a track. In practice, however, how much authors and performers actually receive depends on how rights are contracted away to labels or publishers, the royalty splits negotiated, and whether collection runs through CMOs or direct deals. At the same time, streaming revenue distribution is heavily skewed: major record labels capture massive aggregate daily revenues from global streaming, whereas data from CMOs and per-stream earnings benchmarks show that many individual creators earn modest sums unless they achieve high volume or diversified income.


Streams Needed to Reach the Croatian Minimum Wage

Benchmark Choice (Gross vs. Net)

  • 2026 Croatian Gross Minimum Wage: €1,050.00 per month, effective January 1, 2026 under Croatian government decree (NN 132/2025).
  • Gross vs. Net Selection: This calculation uses the gross minimum wage of €1,050.00/month as a clean, standardized benchmark before employee pension contributions and municipal income tax (net take-home pay is lower—around €799.24/month in Zagreb—and varies by city tax rate and personal allowances; using net pay would lower the required stream count slightly).

Assumptions and Calculation Steps

  1. Per-Stream Payout Estimates: Drawing on streaming royalty estimates cited via Groover and Royalty Exchange (which emphasize that actual payouts fluctuate by listener country, free vs. paid tier, and pro-rata pools):
    • Spotify: $0.003 – $0.005 per stream → Midpoint = $0.00400
    • Apple Music: $0.007 – $0.010 per stream → Midpoint = $0.00850
    • Tidal: $0.012 – $0.015 per stream → Midpoint = $0.01350
  2. Exchange Rate Assumption: Because per-stream estimates are cited in USD while the Croatian minimum wage is in EUR, this model uses a transparent 1:1 reference rate (€1.00 = $1.00).
  3. Distributor Share Assumption: Assumes an independent artist uses a distributor taking a 15% cut on master payouts, leaving 85% (0.85) to the artist (excluding separate publishing/CMO composition payouts, which vary by writing share and reporting cycles).
  4. Formulas:
    • Artist Net per Stream = Midpoint Rate × (1 − 0.15) = Midpoint Rate × 0.85
    • Required Monthly Streams = ⌈ €1,050.00 ÷ Artist Net per Stream ⌉

Opinion on Fairness and Potential Reforms

Streaming has made global music discovery effortless for listeners, yet paying fractions of a cent per play inside pro-rata pools makes earning even a basic minimum wage unrealistic for most independent artists without huge stream totals. Because revenue is divided across master and composition rights, layered intermediaries, and contracts that often concentrate gains among major rights holders, working musicians and songwriters frequently capture a small share of the economic value generated. To make streaming fairer, I would shift subscription distribution toward user-centric or fan-powered models so each listener's payment goes directly to the artists they actually stream. I would also mandate transparent, timely royalty accounting across platforms, distributors, publishers, and societies like HDS ZAMP so creators can trace every play to its payout. Finally, pairing streaming with stronger direct-to-fan monetization and fairer contractual protections for songwriters and performers would help build a more sustainable ecosystem for working creators.
